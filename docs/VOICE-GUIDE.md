# Lorraine's voice guide

How Lorraine K. Lee writes. Use this any time you draft social copy, captions,
newsletter blurbs, or site copy in her voice. It is built from her own LinkedIn
and Instagram posts, so it describes what she actually does, not a generic
"professional but friendly" brand voice.

If a draft does not sound like the samples at the bottom of this file, it is
not ready.

## Who she is on the page

Warm, direct, and useful. A friend who has been through it and is handing you
the thing that worked, not a guru on a stage. She earns trust by going first
with her own mistakes, then turning that into something the reader can do this
week.

Recurring identity anchors she uses as credibility, never as bragging:

- Former LinkedIn employee (and before that, Prezi)
- She was laid off herself, and made a free LinkedIn Learning course after it
- Introvert, stated openly and often, usually with 🙋🏻‍♀️
- Author of Unforgettable Presence® and its RAVE Model
- LinkedIn Learning instructor, speaker, coach

## The core structure

Most of her best posts follow this shape:

1. **A concrete opening line.** One sentence, on its own, then a line break.
   This is the highest-leverage line in the post and the easiest one to get
   wrong, so it has its own rules below. The short version: it states an
   observable fact with a specific in it, usually a number, and it never
   states a feeling.
2. **The setup.** Two or three short lines establishing what she assumed or
   what everyone assumes.
3. **The turn.** The assumption flips. "To my manager, it read as someone
   content exactly where she was." Often a reveal: the colleague in the story
   was her.
4. **The lesson, as one quotable line.** Short, declarative, repeatable.
   "Your work doesn't speak for itself. You have to speak for it."
5. **The small action.** Deliberately tiny and specific, framed as doable this
   week. She says "start small" out loud.
6. **A script or a short list.** See below.
7. **A question back to the reader.** "Which of these have you seen before?
   Let me know in the comments 👇"

Not every post needs all seven. Every post needs 1, 4, and 5.

## The opening line (read this before drafting one)

Her openers are specific first and emotional never. Across her published
posts, eight of eleven carry a number or a named particular within the first
two lines, and not one of them opens by naming how she felt.

What she actually writes:

- "I waited far too long to tell my manager I wanted a promotion." (a named
  action, not "I made a mistake early in my career")
- "For five years, I watched a colleague do everything right." (five years)
- "Last year I posted a job listing and more than 1,000 people applied. / But
  only 10 of them sent me a message on LinkedIn." (two numbers, and the second
  line flips the first)
- "Uber just cut 3,300 jobs and flattened 20% of its management layer."
  (3,300 and 20%)
- "Somewhere last quarter, an opportunity you'd have been perfect for went to
  someone else." (last quarter)
- "I recently asked my team to use Google Slides for a project thinking I was
  making their lives easier. But it did just the opposite!" (the real tool, the
  real task, and the flip inside two sentences)

The test: **could this line open anybody's post?** If yes, it is not hers yet.

Lines that fail the test, and are the exact shape an AI drafts by default:

- "I didn't see it coming."
- "I learned this the hard way."
- "Nobody tells you this, but..."
- "Here's what nobody talks about."
- "It changed everything."
- "I'll never forget the day..."

Each one summarizes a feeling and withholds the detail. Hers do the reverse:
they hand over the detail and let the reader feel it. When the specific is
missing, the fix is never a better adjective, it is going and getting the
actual number, date, tool, or sentence somebody said.

A second pattern worth copying: the two-line opener where line one sets a
number and line two undercuts it ("more than 1,000 people applied" / "only 10
of them sent me a message"). It does the setup and the turn in eleven words.

## Sentence and paragraph rhythm

- Paragraphs are one to three sentences. Many are a single line.
- Blank line between almost every paragraph. The post should look airy on a
  phone.
- Mix long explanatory sentences with very short ones. Fragments are fine and
  she uses them for emphasis.
- Second person throughout. "You," "your manager," "your work."
- Plain words. No corporate jargon, no "leverage," "synergy," "unlock," or
  "at the end of the day."
- Rhetorical question, then her answer, is a favorite move: "My response:
  'There is no magic number, it depends.'"

## Scripts and lists

**Scripts are a signature.** She hands the reader exact words in quotes with
bracketed placeholders, introduced by a line like "A simple script to borrow 👇"
or "Here are two quick scripts you can use:"

> "I want to be considered for [role] in the next cycle. What would you need to
> see from me to support that?"

**Lists are short and emoji-bulleted.** Three or four items, never eight. One
emoji repeated down the list, chosen to fit the post:

- ✅ for action steps
- 🔵 for takeaways or topics covered
- ➡️ for scripts and examples
- 1️⃣ 2️⃣ for numbered announcements
- → for process steps inside a workflow
- Thematic emoji for event recaps (🏎️ 🧸 🎙️ at a conference)

Each bullet is a short phrase, then a colon or period, then a brief plain
explanation. Not a bare keyword.

## Emoji

Used as punctuation for feeling, usually at the end of a line, roughly one per
few paragraphs. Never decorative clusters.

- 😧 😢 😱 for the uncomfortable fact
- 😉 😂 🤦🏻‍♀️ for self-deprecating humor
- 🙋🏻‍♀️ when naming herself (introvert, hand raised)
- 💙 for empathy and community, 🧡 for thanking people by name
- 👇 ⬇️ pointing to comments, a carousel, or a link
- 🫡 ♻️ 🤫 💧 used sparingly and literally

She uses skin-tone-specific emoji for herself. Keep 🙋🏻‍♀️ and 🤦🏻‍♀️ as written.

## Emphasis

- Caps on a single word for spoken stress: "I am SO glad," "this is NOT a good
  idea," "TWO new courses," "a LOT of my past messages."
- Multiple exclamation points when genuinely excited: "My first Dreamforce!! 😍"
  and "(!!!)".
- Parenthetical asides carry the humor and the self-awareness: "(I know,
  probably not the answer you wanted! 😂)", "(introvert here🙋🏻‍♀️!)".

## Her recurring themes

- Visibility beats hard work alone. The overlooked high performer is the
  character she writes for most.
- Your manager cannot advocate for a goal they have never heard.
- Track your wins weekly, in terms the business cares about, not tasks.
- Consistency over frequency in content strategy.
- Introvert-friendly tactics: think first, talk second, protect quiet time,
  embrace JOMO.
- Layoffs and career resilience, always practical and never doom-y.
- AI is a tool she uses openly, but writing that sounds like everyone else is
  the real risk.

## Sponsored and partner posts

She does them, and they still sound like her.

- The brand shows up inside a real problem she had, never as a feature tour.
  She names the actual task ("I gave it a LOT of my past LinkedIn messages")
  and what it told her.
- @mention the brand, thank them by name when they hosted or sponsored.
- Close with an invitation plus link: "Have you tried Gamma's Claude connector
  yet? Check it out here: [link]"
- Disclose with a partner hashtag on its own line at the end:
  #OutreachPartner, #MicrosoftPartner, #gammapartner.

## Hashtags

Very few. On LinkedIn, often zero, or only the partner disclosure tag.
Instagram tolerates more, but keep it to a short relevant set at the end. Never
stuff.

## What she does not do

- Long unbroken paragraphs
- Hustle-culture or fear-mongering framing. Even the layoff posts end in agency
  and a concrete step.
- Generic AI-flavored prose. She has posted publicly about this. No "in today's
  fast-paced world," no "it's not just X, it's Y," no tidy three-part
  rhetorical flourishes stacked back to back.
- Over-polished copy. Her live posts contain small typos and she leaves them.
  Do not sand the writing down until it sounds like nobody wrote it.
- Talking down to the reader, or moralizing.

## Never invent her specifics

Her voice runs on concrete personal detail, which creates a trap: the way to
make a draft sound like her is to add specifics, and the fastest way to add
specifics is to make them up. Do not.

If a draft needs a number, a date, a company, a conversation, or a detail from
her own career that is not already documented (in this file, on the site, or in
something she has said in the conversation), leave a clearly marked blank and
ask her for it. A bracketed [X years] she fills in is fine. A plausible
invention she has to catch is not, and she will not always catch it.

Documented and safe to use: she was laid off and made a free LinkedIn Learning
course afterward, she worked at LinkedIn and Prezi, she is an introvert, she
has lived in SF for nearly 15 years, she wrote Unforgettable Presence®.
Anything more specific than that needs to come from her.

## Note on em dashes

Her social posts use an em dash occasionally. Repo prose, site copy, and
documentation must not, per the style rule in
[AGENTS.md](../AGENTS.md#style-rules). When drafting captions for her to post,
prefer commas, parentheses, or two sentences anyway. It reads more like her
speaking voice.

## Two samples to calibrate against

**The confession opener, full shape:**

> For five years, I watched a colleague do everything right.
>
> Her work was strong and consistent.
>
> And every promotion cycle, she was passed over.😢
>
> She believed her results would speak for themselves. So she kept her head
> down and worked even harder, which made her better at her job and no more
> visible to the people making decisions.
>
> I can tell her story in this much detail because that colleague was me.
>
> [...]
>
> Your work doesn't speak for itself. You have to speak for it.
>
> [...]
>
> If any of this sounds familiar, start small this week. Write down one win and
> include it in your next update to your manager.
>
> Are you already tracking your weekly wins, or is this a habit you need to
> start? Let me know in the comments.👇

**The news hook, with a list and a resource:**

> Uber just cut 3,300 jobs and flattened 20% of its management layer. 😨😨
>
> [...]
>
> If your manager was just laid off, start here:
>
> ✅Make your work visible: it can be as simple as a weekly Slack message in
> your team channel.
> ✅Communicate your impact in terms the business actually cares about.
> ✅Tell people exactly what your goals are, so they know when to advocate for
> you. Nobody can read your mind.
>
> For anyone who's been recently laid off, here is a free LinkedIn Learning
> course I made after my own layoff: [link] 💙
>
> What would you add to the list?
